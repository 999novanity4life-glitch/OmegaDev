# The Importance of Nothing: Using Negative Space in Level Design
- Channel: GDC Festival of Gaming | URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GZ99gAb4T0o | Length: 51:13 | Upload: 2016-07-31 | Captions: auto | Speaker: Jim Brown, Epic Games (2014 session)

- [1:32] "Design is about simplicity... reduction and clarity." Nothing is a tool. As technology lets us render every crack and pore, it's easy to forget the advantage of empty space.
- [2:33] Case study: Facing Worlds (CTF-Face), possibly Epic's most popular map ever. It works because of clean use of space, visual clarity and flow, which come from negative space.
- [4:34] Negative space is the space where things aren't, or the space between objects. It focuses the eye and balances the composition. The brain fills in the missing details.
- [6:34] Negative-space design serves the classic principles of balance, contrast and emphasis.
- [8:35] Use sfumato: define the focal point with surrounding dark space and shadow, not outlines. Commonly used to draw players toward an exit or an important item.
- [9:37] Simple, well-defined shapes that stand out from the background. Tall, narrowing towers anchor the eye on gameplay spaces, and players show up against dark backgrounds.
- [12:44] The counter-example is Call of Duty's favela map: so many paths and details that "there's nowhere for your eye to stop". Gestalt: vision takes in the whole scene at once and groups details into chunks.
- [13:46] The squint test: squinting shows the broad read and hides fine detail. Epic built a squint tool into Unreal Engine 3 that blurs the editor viewport. A Photoshop blur works as a substitute. When blurred, Face still reads (paths, doors, architecture) while favela turns into bleeding colors.
- [16:21] If the foreground and background fight for attention, "neither's going to win". Squint, shrink and blur often.
- [16:51] Palette-knife filter test (merges areas of similar color). Face keeps its paths, doors and assault points; favela turns into blotches.
- [17:56] Figure and ground: clear borders process fastest. If players can't tell figure from ground they struggle to navigate and identify targets.
- [19:51] Gridlock (Gears of War) was the most popular map. The overgrown, aged Gears 2 version lost figure/ground contrast and fell to the bottom of the time-played chart. The Gears 3 version "fell apart" with identical layout, weapons and collision, just from tone and color. A return to a cleaner style put it back in the top 3 most played.
- [23:31] UT2003's Egyptian Face went from 540 actors to over 4,000, and from 130 polygons in view from the sniping towers to over 60,000.
- [24:33] Principle of least effort: minimize cognitive load. More detail is fine, but its placement matters. Decoration broke open areas into "combat closets".
- [25:34] Ask where the players' eyes are going. Ask what you can remove rather than add. Replace detail with a simple change in light or color.
- [26:34] Check the color histogram. Original Face spreads across highs, mids and lows; UT2003 bulges in the midtones, so everything looks uniform.
- [28:39] Shapes direct the eye. Upward-pointing shapes pulled the eye into the sky instead of down to the gameplay.
- [31:15] Quick fixes help a lot: less than a minute of work (lowering the clouds, scaling down the central pyramid) added shadow and contrast.
- [33:17] Navigation complexity is clutter too. Original Face had 59 nav points and about 4 branch points from center to tower; UT2003 had 806 nav points and over a dozen branches.
- [34:47] More choice isn't better. As visual information goes up, comprehension goes down.
- [36:19] Environments grew without players growing, so players got lost visually. Perceived complexity matters more than actual complexity.
- [39:21] Design goals: minimize memory load, reduce attention switching, minimize the inferences players have to make.
- [40:25] Audio needs negative space too.
- [41:59] UT3's Face had 610,000+ polygons in view but still passed the blur, palette and histogram tests, with 144 nav points and 5 branches.
- [45:35] "Subtract the obvious and only add the meaningful."
- [47:40] Q&A: simple spaces make callouts easier ("blue room", "red room"). Ambient effects like blowing papers can be mistaken for threats.

Applies to Roblox how (inferred): Blur or squint at screenshots of each zone and check that paths, entrances, objectives and players stand out from terrain and sky through value contrast. Keep main routes and branch counts low, and spend detail away from combat and navigation space.
