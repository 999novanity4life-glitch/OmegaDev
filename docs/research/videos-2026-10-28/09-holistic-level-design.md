# An Approach to Holistic Level Design
- Channel: GDC Festival of Gaming | URL: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CpOoTAVeEcU | Length: 49:38 | Upload: 2017-07-18 | Captions: auto | Speaker: Steve Lee, Arkane Studios (Dishonored 2 level designer, GDC 2017)

- [2:00] Holistic design means judging every part by how it affects the whole. From 101 Things I Learned in Architecture School: beauty comes more from the "harmonious relationships among the elements" than from the elements themselves.
- [2:30] Split the game into three pillars: presentation (all audio-visual output, including text), gameplay and story. Then design for what emerges between each pair. Gameplay plus presentation gives affordances and intentionality. Presentation plus story gives world building. Gameplay plus story gives interactive narrative.
- [4:02] Affordances (Don Norman, The Design of Everyday Things) mean "form conveying function". A push door with a pull handle misleads people. Good handles work without text in any country.
- [6:03] In first-person levels, affordances are conveyed through layout and lighting, relative to the player's view. Show navigation options against goals and obstacles, and use verticality so players can reach high viewpoints that let them read the situation.
- [6:34] Place interactive objects that offer gameplay: throwable bottles to distract guards, keyholes to peek through, a street speaker the player can shoot down as a distraction.
- [7:05] Make rules consistent. Every Wall of Light connects by a cable to its power source, so players learn to follow the cable to the panel and disable it.
- [7:35] Never use the same assets for non-interactive doors as interactive ones. Non-interactive doors are physically blocked by a recurring visual. The fiction (disease, abandoned buildings) explains why many doors are shut. Set up a clear visual language and stick to it.
- [8:35] Use higher-level affordances to show how big the possibility space is. A non-linear level is wasted if players don't realize it is non-linear.
- [9:05] Put the mission's main choice into the layout. In the Dust District, the two factions sit at one very clear junction, so the choice and how much the mission can differ are visible from that spot.
- [10:06] Sort mission information into three tiers. (1) State the crucial facts plainly. (2) Hint vaguely. (3) Say nothing, so players find it themselves (the Jindosh Lock, hyped as unsolvable and placed near the start).
- [12:06] Promise a lot, then deliver more. Explain enough that players understand the situation, without spoiling exploration or discovery.
- [12:36] "Players don't look up" is a design problem, not human nature. Things that make players look up: stealth and high ground, wall-running, enemies on rooftops, big robots, staircases, cables hanging from ceilings, a pipe pouring water, a lone overhead light in a dark room. If they still don't look up, the rest of the game may have taught them not to.
- [15:09] Intentionality means "making conscious choices with specific goals and expectations in mind". Weak intentionality looks like: being lost, doing things only because the UI says so, pulling a lever because it's a lever, twitch reactions to jump scares.
- [17:12] At Arkane, a challenge or optional puzzle that lacks intentionality gets rejected. Intentionality should be the default state.
- [17:42] Players need four things to act with intent: a choice, motivation (goals), information (clear and consistent affordances), and time to process it.
- [18:12] Give high-level, long-term goals (target across the map, "up to you how") rather than drip-fed tasks like "look here, press this". Let players set the pace and start the action.
- [19:14] The gameplay loop is observe, plan, execute, react, then back to observe. Mobility and verticality give safe high spots for the observe and plan steps.
- [20:45] You don't need stealth for this. Doom 3's teleported monster ambushes kill intentionality. Doom 2 had large areas, let you choose a weapon, and placed enemies facing away from the player.
- [22:18] Linearity feels bad mainly when intentionality is weak. Half-Life 2 and Portal feel less linear because playtesting made what the player wants match what they must do.
- [25:21] World building goals: make the world unique, cohesive and meaningful. Rule 1: be specific. "Future, war, corporations" only establishes a genre. Layer specific details onto archetypes.
- [27:54] Fill districts with scenes that belong to that world and district.
- [29:25] Rule 2: always be world building. Every NPC, objective, side quest and piece of loot is a chance. An empty facade was opened up and dressed as the home of someone evicted, with a note.
- [31:27] Rule 3: world building should say something about people.
- [35:33] "Show, don't tell" is borrowed from passive media. Go further: use interactivity to create narrative intentionality, and evoke drama in the player's mind.
- [38:36] Turn systems into narrative choices. Scripted scenes should be interruptible, with extra lines and scripting that react to the player.
- [40:39] Don't take control away the moment the character is in danger. Cutscenes tell the player "relax".
- [42:11] Half-Life 2's "pick up the can": a physics tutorial that offers obey or rebel, and makes the player side with civilians later.
- [46:14] Great level design depends on how the pillars relate to each other. Make these shared goals across art, writing and direction.

Applies to Roblox how (inferred): Set up one consistent visual language for what can be interacted with (for example ProximityPrompts only on usable doors and objects, with blocked doors always shown using the same prop), and give quests high-level goals with several routes instead of waypoint chains. Dress every building, NPC and loot spot with details specific to that area's story, and use verticality and junctions in the layout to show players their choices.
